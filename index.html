Cutting-edge fashion · research paper

Future Ritualism: Ceremonial Clothing for Imagined Societies

Symbolic ornament · Futuristic regalia · Dramatic ritual dressing. Vaishnavi Bhosale. 1,498 words.

Abstract

Future Ritualism is a speculative fashion direction that imagines how societies yet to exist might express beliefs, hierarchies and collective identities through ceremonial clothing. This paper examines the direction through three contemporary fashion examples: Iris van Herpen’s Earthrise (2021), Anrealage’s Invisible (Spring/Summer 2024), and Yuima Nakazato’s SILENT (Spring/Summer 2026). It analyses symbolism, material experimentation, functionality, cultural responsibility and sustainability. It argues that the direction’s potential lies not simply in futuristic appearance, but in coherent visual languages that communicate the values of imagined cultures.

1. Introduction to the selected direction

Clothing has historically communicated meanings beyond protection and decoration. Ceremonial garments can signify spiritual beliefs, social status, collective identity and transitions between stages of life. Coronation robes, wedding attire, religious vestments and ritual ornaments demonstrate how dress can transform the body into a visible expression of cultural values.

For this research, Future Ritualism is defined as a speculative fashion direction in which designers imagine ceremonial clothing for societies that do not yet exist. It combines the symbolic functions of historical ritual dress with futuristic regalia, sculptural silhouettes, experimental materials and imagined systems of belief. Unlike conventional science-fiction costume, it requires clothing to communicate a coherent cultural narrative rather than merely suggest an advanced technological era.

The direction draws on historical ceremonial dress, speculative world-building and experimental fashion technology. Historical dress offers a foundation for understanding how clothing communicates identity; science fiction allows designers to imagine alternative social structures; and experimental couture provides techniques for translating concepts into physical forms. Crane and Bovone (2006) argue that fashion can be studied as material culture through the creation, communication and interpretation of symbolic value. This framework is relevant because a garment’s meaning depends not only on its appearance, but also on the context in which it is designed, presented and worn.

Future Ritualism is therefore best treated as a proposed design framework, not a formally established fashion movement. Its relevance lies in asking how people might express belonging, reverence, authority and transformation amid technological change and environmental uncertainty.

2.1 Iris van Herpen: Earthrise (2021)

Dutch designer Iris van Herpen combines couture craftsmanship with scientific research, technology and organic forms. Her Earthrise Haute Couture collection, presented in July 2021, explored humanity’s relationship with Earth through a cosmic perspective. The collection’s narrative considered the planet as an interconnected living system (Iris van Herpen, 2021).

A notable garment was created for world-champion skydiver Domitille Kiger. It incorporated thousands of blue spheres arranged in colour gradients, evoking Earth viewed from space. The garment’s movement was revealed during an aerial performance, demonstrating how couture can be developed for an unusual physical environment (Vogue, 2021).

Earthrise is relevant to Future Ritualism because its forms can be interpreted as ceremonial expressions of planetary interconnectedness. Spherical ornament evokes celestial bodies, while movement suggests transformation and transcendence. This is a speculative reading: the collection was not presented as the dress of a fictional civilisation. However, it demonstrates how abstract ideas can be translated into material form through silhouette, ornament, colour and performance.

2.2 Anrealage: Invisible (Spring/Summer 2024)

Japanese designer Kunihiko Morinaga founded Anrealage in 2003. The brand investigates clothing, perception and technology. Its Spring/Summer 2024 collection, Invisible, used photochromic PVC that could shift from transparent to colourful under ultraviolet light. The presentation also explored metamerism, where colour perception changes under different light sources (Anrealage, 2023; Fédération de la Haute Couture et de la Mode, 2023).

This approach transforms clothing from a static surface into a changing visual experience. Instead of relying solely on embroidery or attached ornament, the garments use environmental conditions to influence their appearance. In a speculative ritual context, a garment might reveal a concealed pattern during a ceremony or distinguish participants at different stages of a communal event. Such an application would make ornament interactive. The collection itself should not be misrepresented as a ritual-fashion project; its relevance is its documented experimentation with light and perception.

2.3 Yuima Nakazato: SILENT (Spring/Summer 2026)

Japanese couture designer Yuima Nakazato explores nature, material processes and experimental construction. His Spring/Summer 2026 collection, SILENT, developed from observations of Yakushima Island, including ancient cedar trees, river-smoothed stones and landscapes shaped over long periods (Yuima Nakazato, 2026).

The collection combined digitally printed textiles with ceramic components created through extensive handwork. Epson also supported experiments using recovered textile material and digital printing. Printing transparent ink onto silk organza reduced fraying at cut edges, suggesting that printing can influence construction rather than simply decorate a surface (Epson, 2026).

These processes are relevant to Future Ritualism because materials can carry narratives. Tree rings, geological formations and weathered surfaces could inspire garments that evoke memory, endurance and the relationship between people and the natural environment. For a fictional society that regards geological time as sacred, such forms could become ceremonial regalia. Recovered textiles might also communicate a commitment to resource conservation.

3.1 Design potential and symbolic communication

Future Ritualism’s central design principle is that each visual decision should contribute to the imagined society’s system of meaning. A community organised around celestial observation might use concentric forms and reflective surfaces; a society centred on ecological preservation might favour biomorphic silhouettes and reclaimed materials; and a technologically advanced civilisation might use responsive textiles to distinguish ceremonial roles. World-building gives designers a reason to connect form, ornament and material to a garment’s purpose.

Symbolism nevertheless requires research and coherence. Sacred motifs or culturally specific forms should not be treated as interchangeable decoration. Designers must distinguish between studying existing traditions and inventing new symbolic systems.

3.2 Materials, technology and functionality

Three-dimensional printing, digitally printed textiles, photochromic surfaces and sculptural construction expand the possibilities of ceremonial dress. Yet technical novelty does not automatically create meaningful design. Electronic components can require batteries and maintenance; rigid structures may restrict movement; and elaborate embellishment can increase weight, cost and end-of-life complexity. Ceremonial garments must still accommodate walking, dancing or prolonged standing. Technology should contribute to the concept or function rather than act as an unnecessary signifier of futurism.

3.3 Consumer relevance and sustainability

Potential applications include couture, performance costume, film, immersive exhibitions and experimental presentations. Although highly specialised garments may have a limited direct consumer market, their principles could inform modular accessories, transformable clothing and detachable ornaments. Sustainability must be assessed across production, use, repair and disposal. Recovered textile materials offer one possible approach, but no single material choice proves that a whole garment is sustainable. Durable construction, repairable structures, reusable ornaments and disassembly for material recovery would better align dramatic design with environmental responsibility.

3.4 Cultural responsibility

Ceremonial dress can express belonging and belief, but borrowing sacred motifs or religious forms without understanding their significance risks reducing living traditions to aesthetic decoration. Research, attribution and consultation are essential when designers engage with existing cultural practices. A speculative society should ideally develop its own coherent symbolism rather than rely on superficial combinations of recognisable cultural references.

4. My perspective: a personal reflection

I find Future Ritualism compelling because it positions fashion as a tool for constructing worlds rather than simply responding to trends. I would develop a fictional civilisation that considers humans temporary participants in a larger cosmic ecosystem. Its ceremonial garments could combine spine-like structures, shell-inspired surfaces and translucent elements that evoke water or celestial movement. Detachable ornaments could distinguish different ceremonies or stages of social transformation.

I would begin by defining the society’s beliefs, environment, social structure and important ceremonies before developing a visual code for colour, silhouette, ornament and material. I would experiment with sculpted textiles, recovered materials and lightweight fabricated components, prioritising movement, repair and reconfiguration. Technology would be used only when it supported the garment’s symbolic or functional purpose.

A weakness of this direction is that futuristic fashion can look impressive without developing a convincing conceptual foundation. Metallic surfaces, exaggerated headpieces and dramatic silhouettes may suggest science fiction, but they do not automatically create a meaningful ritual system. Without a clear relationship between form and narrative, the result risks becoming costume without context. I am also concerned that elaborate one-off garments can create waste. Detachable components, reusable ornaments and repairable structures would allow visual complexity without requiring every ceremonial object to be entirely new.

Ultimately, I believe Future Ritualism can expand fashion’s role in speculative design and couture. Its value lies not simply in predicting what people might wear in the future, but in asking what future societies might choose to celebrate, protect, remember and believe in.

5. Conclusion

Future Ritualism provides a framework for exploring ceremonial dress, speculative world-building, technological experimentation and symbolic communication. Earthrise, Invisible and SILENT demonstrate relevant possibilities through sculptural couture, transformative surfaces and material innovation. These collections are not a formally established Future Ritualism movement, but they provide credible starting points for developing its visual and conceptual language. Its future potential depends on uniting imaginative aesthetics with coherent symbolism, practical construction, cultural awareness and responsible material choices.
